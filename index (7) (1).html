EcoFinder

Find out what lives anywhere on Earth

EcoFinder shows you which animals have been seen near any spot on the planet, from your backyard to the open ocean, on a satellite map.

How to use it

  1. Pick a place. Open the Map screen, then click anywhere on the satellite map or type a place name in the search box.
  2. Adjust the search. Choose a radius (5 to 250 km) and, if you like, a group such as birds, mammals or fish. Turn on Hide bugs to remove insects, spiders and similar creatures, using each species' scientific classification.
  3. Browse the list. Each species shows a photo, its common and scientific names, and how many sightings each source has. Links lead to National Geographic, Wikipedia and more.
  4. Find a specific animal. Type a name like "emperor penguin" or "great white shark" in the Find any animal box on the Map screen to shade everywhere it has been recorded.
  5. Match a photo. Open the Photo screen, add your picture, say where it was taken, and compare it with the animals recorded there. No AI guessing, and your photo stays on your device.
  6. Keep a catalog. Open the Catalog screen to add animals and what you know about them. You can auto-fill the details, add your own photo, and export your list.
  7. Look it up. Open the Ask screen to search what biologists do and the tools they use. Summaries come from Wikipedia, with links to search Britannica.

Good to know

  • The list shows animals people have recorded, not every animal that is there. Remote areas have fewer records.
  • Sources overlap, so a species' counts should be read per source, not added together.
  • On the Photo screen, you make the final call by comparing your picture with recorded animals. Look-alike species are easy to confuse, so never touch, handle or eat anything based on a photo match.
  • The Ask screen shows Wikipedia summaries (CC BY-SA 4.0) and links to Britannica, which has no free public feed, so some of its articles may need an account. The bug filter looks up each species' scientific class, so no AI is involved anywhere on the site, and photos you add on the Photo screen never leave your device.
  • EcoFinder is an independent project and is not run by or affiliated with the organizations listed on the Credits screen.

Maps, names and reference

Satellite imagery is from Esri, place search is by OpenStreetMap, and common names come from iNaturalist. The Ask screen shows summaries from Wikipedia and links to Britannica. The bug filter uses scientific classification from GBIF, with no AI.

Wikipedia text is available under the CC BY-SA 4.0 license. Every summary on the Ask screen links back to its article.

EcoFinder is an independent project and is not run by or affiliated with any of the organizations listed here.

      OBIS lists ocean species by number of records (animals only). The Animal Telemetry Network count is the number of tagged-animal deployments with a tracked fix inside the box; its public data cover older US satellite tags, so many places will have none. NOAA's National Ocean Service has no wildlife lookup of its own. Its marine science programs (coastal science centers, marine sanctuaries, and the Integrated Ocean Observing System that runs the telemetry network) publish into OBIS-USA, the US national node of OBIS, which is what the "NOAA & US federal marine data" source reads. That node also holds data from other agencies such as USGS and NMFS, so it is not NOS-only, and it is mostly US waters. Each species also links to the Ocean Service website. Two sources have no public data feed, so they are links rather than live results: Wildlife Insights (camera-trap data, free account needed to download) and Map of Life Regions (expected and recorded species for any area you draw).
